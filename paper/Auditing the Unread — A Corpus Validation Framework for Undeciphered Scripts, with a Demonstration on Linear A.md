## Abstract

Quantitative work on undeciphered scripts inherits two hazards that are rarely
made explicit: the corpus may carry interpretive contamination its users cannot
see, and the statistic may be beyond what the corpus can support. This paper
develops a general audit and adequacy-testing framework for such corpora and
demonstrates it on Linear A.

Three results follow. First, a widely used digital Linear A corpus systematically
inverts the signs AB21 (QI/OVIS, sheep) and AB22 (CAP, goats) across fourteen
documents; an independent third witness agrees with the alternative reading at
fifteen of fifteen attestable sites, in both directions, establishing a
mis-mapping rather than a scholarly disagreement. Second, three adequacy tests
show that the standard routes to a structural fingerprint are closed for this
corpus: minimal pairs fall *below* chance (z = −1.63), conditional entropy is
inestimable by two orders of magnitude, and vowel-sequencing dependency is
undetectable under either a five- or three-vowel specification, with harmony of
strength ≥ 0.10 excluded by power calibration. Third, a replication of Packard's
(1974) statistical defense of the transfer of Linear B phonetic values to Linear
A — the first in fifty-two years — reproduces his weak evidence (internal
alternations, 1.55 : 1, p = 0.17) and independently confirms his strong evidence
(Knossos name parallels, 4.74 : 1, z = +4.34, p = 0.0020) on a different corpus
with four thousand times his permutation count.

A positive structural finding survives null testing: affixation exceeds chance in
both registers (z = +5.91 administrative, +6.74 religious), while the
prefix/suffix *ratio* does not (z = +0.23), which means the direction of
affixation carries no information at this corpus size and cannot bear on
language-family arguments.

Four times during this work a defect in the comparison tooling produced what
looked like a substantive finding, once at conventional significance. The
methodological conclusion is that in this domain a divergence count measures the
comparator at least as much as the corpus, and that positive results from
automated collation require manual inspection of the driving documents before
they can be reported.

**Keywords:** Linear A, corpus validation, reproducibility, undeciphered scripts,
permutation testing, adequacy testing, Packard 1974, SigLA

## 1. Introduction

> **In brief.** Quantitative studies of undeciphered scripts depend on digital
> corpora whose editorial layers are usually invisible to their users, and on
> statistics whose data requirements are usually unstated. This paper supplies an
> audit method for the first problem and an adequacy protocol for the second, and
> reports what both produce when applied to Linear A.

Linear A is the administrative and religious script of Neopalatial Crete,
attested in roughly fifteen hundred documents from the eighteenth to the
fifteenth century BCE. It remains undeciphered. The language behind it, generally
called Minoan, has no demonstrated affiliation, and successive proposals —
Semitic, Anatolian, Hurrian, Tyrrhenian — have failed to command assent.

Because decipherment has stalled, the field's center of gravity has shifted
toward statistical and computational description: sign frequencies, positional
distributions, structural characteristics, and recurrent associations among
sign-pairs and sign-sequences. That shift is sensible. It also creates a
dependency that has not been examined with the seriousness it deserves.

Statistical description requires a machine-readable corpus. Machine-readable
Linear A corpora are compilations, and compilation involves editorial decisions:
which signs are read, where words divide, which readings are certain, how
variants are named, what numeric value a fraction sign carries. Each decision is
defensible; each is also a claim. When a corpus is consumed as a flat table of
sign identifiers, those claims become invisible, and a downstream statistic
inherits them without the means to interrogate them.

This is not hypothetical. Section 4 documents a corpus in wide use that inverts
sheep and goats across fourteen documents, and a second in which the marking of
doubtful readings is absent from every layer, so that a sign an editor could
barely make out is indistinguishable from one they read with confidence.

The second problem is independent of the first. Several statistics standard in
the decipherment literature have data requirements that are rarely stated and, on
this corpus, are not met. A conditional entropy figure can be computed from any
corpus; whether it measures the script or the sampling is a separate question,
answerable by counting. Section 6 shows that for Linear A the answer is sampling,
by a margin of two orders of magnitude.

### 1.1 What this paper does

It develops an audit framework with four components — a governing principle for
ingestion, structured preservation of editorial uncertainty, a multi-witness
comparison harness, and an adequacy protocol that asks whether a statistic is
computable before computing it — and applies them to Linear A.

The demonstration produces one corpus correction, two resolved defects, a triage
of a hundred and thirty-seven logged disagreements down to nine, three bounded
negative results, one positive structural finding, and a replication.

### 1.2 What this paper does not do

It offers no decipherment and no reading of any Linear A text. It makes no claim
about the affiliation of the Minoan language; Section 7 explains why the measures
that could bear on that question are demonstrably beyond this corpus.

Where the work bears on published positions it does so narrowly and, in the case
of Packard, in his favor. The reader should be warned in advance that the
critical findings here are directed at *citation practice and corpus
infrastructure*, not at the scholarship those rest on.

## 2. Prior work and position

> **In brief.** The corpus tradition runs GORILA → Douros → digital editions;
> the statistical tradition runs Packard 1974 → Pope & Raison 1978 → present-day
> computational work. This paper sits at their intersection and audits the
> junction.

### 2.1 The corpus tradition

The standard edition is GORILA (Godart & Olivier 1976–1985), five volumes of
photographs, facsimiles and transcriptions. GORILA marks uncertain readings with
subscript dots and records breaks and erasures. It is the foundation of
everything that follows.

Downstream of GORILA, the material has been re-encoded several times: Raison and
Pope's *Corpus transnuméré*, Brice's edition, Younger's phonetic transcription,
Douros's tabulation, van Soesbergen's corpus, and the SigLA palaeographical
database of Salgarella and Castellan. Each re-encoding serves a purpose and each
loses or adds something. The losses are the subject of Section 4.

### 2.2 The statistical tradition

The practice of transliterating Linear A with Linear B sound values rests on an
argument made by Packard (1967, 1974) and refined by Pope and Raison (1978).
Packard constructed nine deliberately invalid "random decipherments" as a control
and asked whether the true Linear B values produced more confirmatory evidence
than the fictitious ones. He reported a ratio "just over 2 : 1" in favor of the
Linear B values.

That figure has circulated ever since as the warrant for backward projection. It
has not, to this author's knowledge, been re-run. Section 8 re-runs it.

### 2.3 Position within digital humanities

The argument of this paper belongs to a discussion wider than Aegean studies:
that a digital corpus is an argument rather than a record, and that its editorial
decisions constitute claims which downstream users inherit without seeing.

Critical work in digital humanities has made this point about selection — whose
texts are digitized — and about markup, where encoding schemes embed
interpretive commitments. The case of undeciphered scripts sharpens it, for two
reasons.

First, the interpretive layer is unusually thick relative to the evidence. A
transliteration of a Linear A tablet embeds a sign inventory, a set of phonetic
values borrowed from another language, a word division, and a judgment about
which readings are certain. None is neutral, and §4 shows that in current corpora
several are not even recoverable.

Second, the usual corrective is unavailable. A user of a digitized Greek corpus
can read the Greek. A user of a Linear A corpus cannot check a reading against
the language, because the language is unknown. The corpus is therefore not merely
a convenience but the entire evidential basis, and its defects are invisible from
inside.

This is why the framework here is an *audit* rather than an edition. It does not
propose a better corpus; it proposes a method for establishing what any given
corpus can and cannot support, and for making its editorial layers legible to the
statistics that consume them. The divergence register is the instrument of that
legibility: it converts an editorial disagreement from something a corpus hides
into something a downstream analysis can condition on.

### 2.4 The theoretical objection

Steele and Meißner (2017) treat backward projection as a problem rather than a
settled procedure. Their case is that a script adapted to a different language
routinely has its sign values reassigned — Latin script for Turkish or
Vietnamese, or the Cherokee syllabary, whose eighty-five signs are mostly based
on Latin letters and of which none retains its Latin value.

It should be recorded that this objection is not a modern revision. Packard
himself opens his chapter on the phonetic values by calling the inference from
identity of shape to identity of value "a natural but highly uncertain
hypothesis", presses the Cypriot parallel, cites the Cherokee case, and states
that his use of conventional transliteration "does not imply endorsement of the
phonetic values". The author of the study now used to license the practice was
its earliest skeptic.

## 3. Method

> **In brief.** Four components: sign identity as the unit of data; structured
> preservation of uncertainty; comparison against multiple independent witnesses
> with conventions folded first; and an adequacy check before any statistic is
> computed. All procedures are seeded and reproduce byte-identically from source.

### 3.1 The governing principle

> **The Unicode sign identity is the data. Every ASCII label is a lossy
> derivative and is regenerated from the sign identifier.**

Digital Linear A corpora typically carry two parallel encodings: the signs
themselves, as Unicode codepoints in the Linear A block, and a human-readable
transliteration such as `KU-RO` or `GRA+K+L`. The second is convenient and is
what analysis code tends to read. It is also lossy, and the losses are not random.

Adopting the sign identifier as authoritative resolved three of the first four
defects encountered here at a stroke (Section 4.1). It also makes cross-witness
comparison tractable, because sign numbers are shared across editions where
labels are not.

### 3.2 Preserving uncertainty

Editorial uncertainty is data. This framework requires it to survive ingestion as
structured fields rather than as punctuation inside a string:

- **damage**: whether material is missing before, after or within a token, and a
  derived `complete` flag
- **reading certainty**: whether the editor was confident of the sign
- **measure confidence**: for values that are derived or conjectured rather than
  demonstrated, a grade and a citation

The consequence of not doing this is quantified in Section 4.1: treating damaged
fragments as whole words would have inflated the lexicon by 374 phantom types,
thirty percent.

### 3.3 Multi-witness comparison

A corpus cannot be validated against itself. This framework compares against
independent witnesses, folding documented convention equivalences *before*
counting divergences, and logging every residual disagreement in a machine-readable
register with the evidence that would settle it.

Three witnesses are used here: Younger's phonetic transcription (tabular and
free-text records, compared separately), and the SigLA palaeographical database.

The register is the methodological core. Its rule is: **adopt the source reading
consistently, never silently patch, log everything so results can be re-run under
either reading.** Section 4.2 shows why deferral rather than correction is the
right default: had the AB21/AB22 conflict been "fixed" when first encountered as
a single site, the evidence that it was *systematic* — and therefore a mapping
error rather than a disagreement — would have been destroyed.

### 3.4 Adequacy testing

Before computing a statistic, determine whether the corpus can support it. The
protocol is simple counting: how many cells does the estimator have, how many
observations are available per cell, and what is the standard requirement.

For conditional entropy over a k-sign inventory the table has k² cells. For
Linear A, k = 272 and the within-word bigram count is 1875, giving 0.025
observations per cell. No estimator recovers information that is not present.

Where a null result is reported, its sensitivity is established by injecting
synthetic signal of known strength and measuring the detection rate. A negative
without a power calibration is uninformative.

### 3.5 Null models

Every quantitative claim in this paper is tested against a null model that
preserves the structure the claim is *not* about. For sign-distribution claims,
types are resampled preserving length distribution and positional unigram
frequencies. For value-assignment claims, values are permuted among signs
preserving marginal frequencies. For register comparisons, labels are permuted at
the level of the **document**, not the word, because words within a document share
a scribe, a document type and a subject and are not independent.

That last point is not a technicality. The register length difference was
originally tested word-wise and returned p = 6.2 × 10⁻¹⁸; the correct
record-level permutation returns z = +10.21 on the same data. The finding
survives, but the original figure was pseudo-replicated and unusable.

### 3.6 A guide to the statistical methods used

This section is for readers without a quantitative background. Nothing in the
paper requires more than the following.

**Permutation test.** Rather than assume a distribution, shuffle the data in a
way that destroys the pattern being claimed while preserving everything else,
many times, and see where the real value falls in the resulting spread. If it
falls inside, the pattern is what chance produces. Every claim here is tested
this way, which avoids assuming distributions the data may not have.

**z-score.** How many standard deviations the observed value sits from the
shuffled average. Roughly: |z| under 2 is unremarkable, 2–3 is suggestive, above
4 is strong. Reported throughout in preference to p-values, because a p-value
floored by the number of shuffles can mislead.

**p-value.** The proportion of shuffles at least as extreme as the observed
value. Where 4000 shuffles are run the smallest reportable p is about 0.0002, so
"p = 0.0005" means "none of the shuffles beat it", not a precise probability.

**Null model.** The specification of what gets shuffled. This carries most of
the intellectual content: a null that preserves too little makes anything look
significant. Here, sign frequencies and word lengths are always preserved,
because a result that only reflects those is not a result about structure.

**Multiple comparisons.** Testing twenty things at the conventional threshold
produces about one false positive by construction. Benjamini-Hochberg correction
adjusts the threshold to control that rate.

**Power and adequacy.** Whether a test *could* have detected an effect had one
been present, established by injecting a known effect and measuring the
detection rate. A negative result without this is uninterpretable.

**Entropy.** Average information per sign, in bits. Higher means less
predictable. Sensitive to sample size, so figures from differently sized corpora
are not comparable without correction.

### 3.7 Policy on multiple comparisons

Correction is applied where a family of tests is scanned for the largest effect,
and not where a single pre-specified hypothesis is tested. Stated explicitly,
because the paper is inconsistent on the surface and the inconsistency is
deliberate:

- **§5.4 positional preference: corrected.** Forty-two signs are tested and the
  survivors reported. This is a scan; without correction roughly two signs would
  appear significant by construction. Benjamini-Hochberg is applied across every
  sign attested fifteen times or more.
- **§5.2 register length, §5.3 register entropy: not corrected.** One
  pre-specified hypothesis each, formulated before the data were examined.
- **§5.5 affixation: not corrected.** Two registers reported separately rather
  than pooled and scanned; the direction test is a single pre-specified
  comparison. Both survive at |z| > 5.9 regardless.
- **§8 Packard: not corrected.** Positional categories are reported separately
  because Packard reports them separately, and the headline figures are the
  totals, which are single tests. Applying correction across his three positions
  would not change any conclusion: the alternation result fails to reach
  significance uncorrected, and the name-parallel result survives at z = +4.34
  on the Knossos-restricted criterion (z = +5.51 unrestricted).

The general rule: where this paper reports "n of m survived", correction has been
applied; where it reports a single figure, it has not.

### 3.8 Deferral rather than correction

The register policy of §3.3 is to log disagreements and *not* resolve them at the
point of discovery. This is worth defending, because the instinct in corpus work
is the opposite.

The epistemic difference is between an **adjudication** and a **correction**. An
adjudication is a claim that one reading is right, made on evidence, and it
requires a witness independent of both parties. A correction is a change to the
data, and it destroys the record of what was there before.

Correcting at the point of discovery collapses these. It makes each disagreement
a local judgment call, and local judgment calls do not aggregate: fourteen
independent decisions to prefer one editor over another leave no trace of the
fact that the fourteen were the same decision.

That trace was the finding. D4 was discovered as a single conflict on one tablet,
and at that moment there was no basis to prefer either reading. Only the
*pattern* — every instance inverted, in both directions, without exception —
established a mapping error rather than a scholarly disagreement. Had the first
instance been corrected, the pattern would never have formed.

The cost of deferral is that the corpus knowingly contains readings believed to
be wrong, for as long as it takes to adjudicate them. That cost is real and is
paid in `divergences.json`, which is why every entry carries the evidence that
would settle it. The benefit is that the corpus remains a record of a
disagreement rather than the residue of one.

### 3.9 Reproducibility

All random procedures are seeded (20260731). The pipeline runs end to end from
the raw source through sixteen stages via a single script; `corpus_v1.json` and
its derivatives rebuild byte-identically. Code, documentation and the divergence
register are available at the repository cited above. Data licenses differ by
component and are set out there.

## 4. The corpus audit

> **In brief.** Applying the framework to a widely used digital Linear A corpus
> found thirteen defects. Three shared a single cause and were fixed by one rule.
> One was a systematic sign inversion affecting fourteen documents, confirmed by
> a third witness at fifteen of fifteen sites. One is structural and could only
> be resolved by importing a different corpus. Three sweeps against independent
> witnesses give 92.0%, 94.7% and 95.9% token agreement.

### 4.1 Provenance and the first four defects

The corpus used here derives from a public digital edition whose sign
transcriptions come from GORILA, whose word division and tabulation come from
Douros, and whose commentary comes from Younger. Only the GORILA layer is
peer-reviewed. The extraction pipeline between them is unreviewed and contributes
its own error surface.

Four defects were found on first inspection.

**D1. Damage markers stripped from the label layer.** The break marker survives
in the sign layer but is deleted from the transliteration, affecting 990 of 3612
sign-groups. Any analysis reading the label layer treats fragments as whole
words. The cost is quantifiable: distinct sign-group types fall from 1247 to 873
once damaged tokens are excluded, so ignoring the flag would have inflated the
lexicon by **374 phantom types**, or thirty percent.

**D2. Fraction signs pre-converted to numeric values.** Sign A703 was rendered
`1/5` and A706 `1/6`, values the reference literature explicitly marks as
surmised. Conjecture had been hardened into data.

**D3. Variant indices dropped in labels.** `GRA+K+L` for a sign whose GORILA
definition is GRA+K+**L2**; VINa flattened to `VIN` while VINb survived.

**D4. A sign-reading conflict** at HT 20: the source reads AB21 (QI/OVIS, sheep)
where Younger reads AB22 (CAP, goats).

D1, D2 and D3 turned out to be three symptoms of one cause. Analysis was drawing
on the ASCII label rather than the sign identifier. Adopting the governing
principle of §3.1 resolved all three: the L2 index, for instance, was never lost
from the data at all — sign A584's GORILA definition *is* GRA+K+L2, and only the
rendering had dropped it.

D2 was subsequently resolved outright by adopting the fraction system of Corazza
et al. (2021), derived by constraint programming and superseding the conjectural
grades. That adoption produced **D13**: a value this project had graded "secure"
on the standard reference's authority, K = 1/16, is given as 1/10 in the current
literature as part of a decimal-sexagesimal subset. The "secure" grade had come
from a summary rather than from checking whether the value was still held.

### 4.2 D4: a systematic inversion

D4 entered the register as a single conflict on one tablet. Three stages
established what it actually was.

**Stage one: the sweep.** An automated comparison against Younger's tabular
records (411 documents) showed the conflict was not isolated. AB21 and AB22 are
read differently at **eighteen sites across fourteen documents**, spanning Haghia
Triada, Khania, Knossos, Phaistos and Zakros, and the disagreement runs in *both
directions*.

**Stage two: exclusion of a palaeographic explanation.** If contested readings
concentrated in particular scribal hands, the dispute would be partly about
confusable letterforms. They do not. Only five of the eighteen sites carry a
scribal attribution and those five fall under five different scribes.

**Stage three: a third witness.** The SigLA database, when decoded (§4.4), agrees
with Younger at **fifteen of fifteen attestable sites, in both directions,
without exception**.

A perfect inversion across fifteen independent sites is not fifteen misreadings.
It is a systematic mis-mapping in the tabulation. The inversion is now applied at
build time and the corpus reproduces SigLA on twelve of twelve documents.

The methodological consequence deserves emphasis. The register policy of §3.3 is
to retain the source reading and defer adjudication. Had the conflict been
"corrected" toward either witness when first encountered as a single site, the
pattern that proved the source wrong would have been erased. **Deferral was not
caution; it was what made the finding possible.**

Two qualifications. SigLA does not record the gender variants the source carries,
so adjudication is at AB21/AB22 family level. Two documents are absent from SigLA
and remain unadjudicated.

### 4.3 D12: uncertainty absent from every layer

D1 concerned a marker present in one layer and stripped from another; it was
recoverable. D12 is worse.

GORILA marks uncertain sign readings with subscript dots. Younger uses
underlining. The corpus used here carries **neither, in any layer**. A sign an
editor could barely make out is indistinguishable from one read with confidence.

The defect was detected indirectly. Comparing fraction-sign counts against
Corazza et al., most signs tracked closely — sign A707 is attested exactly 137
times in both — but sign A709 showed ten occurrences here against their one.
Their footnote lists the doubtful instances they exclude, and our ten fall
precisely in the documents they name. We were counting their excluded doubtful
readings as certain.

This is not bookkeeping. Corazza et al. build a statistical argument at
p = 4 × 10⁻¹² that A709 is *not an independent sign*, and that argument rests on
its rarity. On uncorrected counts the sign is ten times commoner and the argument
would not go through.

### 4.4 Recovering a witness that carries certainty

D12 cannot be fixed by better processing of the same source; it requires a corpus
that records certainty natively. SigLA does.

SigLA ships its corpus as two OCaml `Marshal` blobs escaped as decimal octets
inside JavaScript string literals. `Marshal` stores records *positionally*, so
field names are absent from the file and the data cannot be located by text
search. Decoding required implementing the format: header parsing, the small-int,
small-string and small-block prefix encodings, the sharing table for
back-references, and the block, string, double and custom codes.

The record layout was then recovered structurally and cross-checked against the
publicly available import source, which defines the label type as carrying a
boolean `unsure` alongside the sign and its metadata.

Yield: **802 documents, 5144 attestations**, of which 686 documents overlap the
1721 records used here. Of the attestations, 4712 are marked confident, **44
doubtful**, 388 unreadable or unclassified, with 104 erasures and 7 ghosts. That
is 8.4% of attestations in categories the working corpus cannot express.

### 4.5 Three sweeps

| sweep | witness            | scope                         | documents | tokens | agreement |
|-------|--------------------|-------------------------------|-----------|--------|-----------|
| 1     | Younger, tabular   | administrative                | 411       | 3644   | **92.0%** |
| 2     | Younger, free-text | non-tabular incl. religious   | 994       | 1167   | **94.7%** |
| 3     | SigLA              | all overlapping, syllabograms | 657       | 4105   | **95.9%** |

Sweep 2 falls to **80.6%** on the libation subset specifically, which is the
genre carrying the most weight for grammatical analysis and the one where the
corpus is least reliable.

Sweep 3 required care. Comparing all signs gives 91.8%; comparing syllabograms
only gives 95.9%. The gap is not disagreement but coverage: SigLA records 332
fraction tokens against 447 here, and sign A732 is absent entirely. A
palaeographical database indexes drawn sign forms, so composite fractions are
plausibly drawn as components or not drawn. **SigLA must therefore not be used to
adjudicate fraction counts**, which matters because D13 concerns exactly that.

A hypothesis that did not survive: after the KN Zc 6 case (§4.6) it seemed
plausible that disagreements might concentrate in readings SigLA flags as
doubtful. Corpus-wide they do not. Restricting to confident signs makes agreement
slightly *worse*, 95.9% to 95.2%. D12 is a real defect, but it does not explain
the residual divergence.

### 4.6 Triage: 137 disagreements to 9

The two long tails logged by the Younger sweeps totaled 137 sites. Eighty-four
were testable against SigLA — sign-type conflicts in covered documents.

| outcome                                         | n     |
|-------------------------------------------------|-------|
| identical after normalisation                   | 14    |
| identical after convention folding              | 18    |
| near-identical, tokenisation or annotation only | 9     |
| never a conflict (source and Younger agree)     | 14    |
| SigLA adjudicates, backs the source             | 11    |
| SigLA adjudicates, backs Younger                | 2     |
| ambiguous or absent in SigLA                    | 7     |
| **genuine sign conflict**                       | **9** |

**The long tail was inflated roughly nine-fold by convention.** Most sites
resolve to the same sign under two naming systems — HIDE = \*180, MU = \*23,
SO = \*363, PU3 = \*314 — or to ligature tokenization, or to annotation markers.

**Where SigLA can adjudicate a single-sign conflict it backs the source eleven
times to two.** This is the opposite of D4 and must be stated, because D4 invites
the inference that the source is unreliable. It is not. It had one systematic
error, now corrected, and on the residual it is right more often than the
comparison witness.

Nine genuine conflicts remain and require plate collation.

One partial resolution came from the same exercise. KN Zc 6 is the only contested
word-division site SigLA covers. SigLA records **ten** signs on the relevant line,
with position seven **unreadable**, sitting exactly at the disputed boundary.
Neither the source nor Younger records a sign there at all. The disagreement is
therefore not two editors reading the same marks differently: there is an
illegible sign at the contested point, which one editor appears to have treated
as a divider and the other to have ignored. This is D12 surfacing inside a
word-division dispute — a flattened uncertainty presented as a confident reading
by both witnesses, in opposite directions.

## 5. Structural results

> **In brief.** Registers differ in word length and per-sign entropy, both
> confirmed by permutation at the document level. Affixation exceeds chance in
> both registers. The prefix/suffix *ratio* does not, which withdraws a published
> argument about language typology in both directions. Roughly half the
> positional preferences survive multiple-comparison correction.

### 5.1 Genre separation

Genre was assigned mechanically from physical support, with no interpretation:
clay administrative documents (tablets, nodules, roundels, sealings, bars) against
Z-type supports (stone, clay and metal vessels, architecture, objects). 1517
records administrative, 204 religious, none unclassified.

All structural analysis uses complete, undamaged tokens only, and positional and
affix work uses multi-sign groups only: a fragment's first sign is not evidence
of word-initial position, and a single-sign token is trivially both initial and
final. This leaves 767 administrative and 160 religious multi-sign groups.

### 5.2 The registers are structurally distinct

Mean sign-group length is 2.81 administrative against 4.06 religious. Groups of
six or more signs number 25 of 160 in the religious corpus against 7 of 767 in
the administrative.

The word-level Mann-Whitney test returns p = 6.2 × 10⁻¹⁸ and **is not usable**:
it treats every word as independent when words within a document share a scribe,
a document type and a subject. Re-tested by permuting genre labels across records
(259 administrative, 99 religious, 5000 trials), the observed difference of
+1.249 signs sits against a null of −0.001 ± 0.122, **z = +10.21**. The finding
survives at full strength; the original figure was pseudo-replicated.

### 5.3 Entropy, and what it cannot do

Unigram entropy over complete sign-group tokens:

| corpus         | n    | k   | H1 (MLE) | Miller-Madow | Chao-Shen | max   |
|----------------|------|-----|----------|--------------|-----------|-------|
| whole          | 4534 | 272 | 6.281    | 6.326        | 6.374     | 8.087 |
| administrative | 3813 | 262 | 6.272    | 6.324        | 6.375     | 8.033 |
| religious      | 721  | 93  | 5.614    | 5.706        | 5.732     | 6.539 |

Three estimators agree within 0.1 bits. Bootstrap interval on the whole-corpus
MLE: 6.179–6.291.

These figures **must not be compared to published entropy values for known
scripts.** Entropy estimates are biased by sample size and published values come
from corpora orders of magnitude larger; a valid comparison requires subsampling
the reference to matched n.

The register comparison illustrates the point. Naively, religious 5.61 against
administrative 6.27 looks like a large difference; it is also confounded by
different n and different k. Subsampling the administrative corpus to the
religious n gives 6.275 ± 0.070 against the religious 5.732, **z = −7.76**,
p = 0.000 over 400 trials. The religious corpus is genuinely lower-entropy by
about half a bit.

That is independent confirmation, by an unrelated method, of the formulaic
structure found in §5.6, and it sharpens §5.2: the religious register has
*longer* words but *less* information per sign, which is what repetition of a
fixed formula predicts and richer vocabulary would not.

### 5.4 Positional preference

Signs were tested for edge preference against a within-word permutation null that
shuffles sign order inside each word — preserving word lengths and each word's
sign multiset, destroying only arrangement — with Benjamini-Hochberg correction
across every sign attested fifteen times or more.

| register       | signs tested | surviving correction |
|----------------|--------------|----------------------|
| administrative | 42           | **24**               |
| religious      | 14           | **3**                |

The strong effects hold comfortably: AB002 is final in 64 of 70 occurrences
against 32.0 expected; AB008 initial in 71 of 76 against 24.4. But roughly half
the table does not survive, and those signs should not be cited.

Two cautions. AB008 is the pure-vowel sign, and in a CV syllabary vowel-only signs
cluster word-initially for phonotactic reasons, so its skew is probably
orthographic rather than morphological. And a pattern that looked striking — sign
A301 occurring fifteen times, every one medial — does **not** survive permutation:
in words of that length a medial-only distribution is unremarkable.

### 5.5 Affixation: real, but directionless

An affix relation was defined conservatively: type B is affixed if it equals an
independently attested type A plus one or two signs at one edge, with the stem
itself attested as a complete word.

|                             | administrative | religious  |
|-----------------------------|----------------|------------|
| distinct types              | 519            | 142        |
| types with an attested stem | 75 (14.5%)     | 18 (12.7%) |
| affix relations             | 80             | 21         |
| prefix                      | 34 (42%)       | 8 (38%)    |
| suffix                      | 46 (58%)       | 13 (62%)   |

Tested against types resampled preserving length distribution and positional
frequencies (300 trials):

|                             | observed | null          | z         |
|-----------------------------|----------|---------------|-----------|
| admin, total relations      | 80       | 37.2 ± 7.2    | **+5.91** |
| religious, total relations  | 21       | 4.4 ± 2.5     | **+6.74** |
| admin, **prefix share**     | 0.425    | 0.405 ± 0.086 | **+0.23** |
| religious, **prefix share** | 0.381    | 0.302 ± 0.268 | **+0.29** |

**Affixation is a genuine structural property**, exceeding chance by roughly six
standard deviations in both registers, with prefix and suffix relations each
exceeding chance independently. This is the only positive structural finding
here that survives null testing; minimal pairs, by contrast, fall *below* chance
(§6.1).

**The prefix/suffix ratio is not a finding.** Observed prefix share sits within a
quarter of a standard deviation of the null in both registers. The chance
baseline is itself about 0.40 given the positional distribution of signs, so the
observed 42% is exactly what randomness produces.

**How much directional preference would be detectable?** A ratio at chance level
admits two readings: the language has no directional preference, or the method
cannot detect one at this corpus size. These are different claims, and the
difference decides whether §5.5 says anything about Linear A at all. It is
answerable by injection.

Synthetic corpora were built at the observed size and length distribution with a
known affixation direction, and the same measure and null model applied.

| true P(prefix) | observed  | null  | z         | relations | detection rate  |
|----------------|-----------|-------|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| 0.50           | 0.481     | 0.493 | −0.07     | 76        | **0 / 25 (0%)** |
| **0.59**       | **0.576** | 0.512 | **+0.31** | 74        | **0 / 25 (0%)** |
| 0.65           | 0.638     | 0.504 | +0.67     | 75        | 0 / 25 (0%)     |
| 0.75           | 0.730     | 0.518 | +1.05     | 72        | 0 / 25 (0%)     |
| 0.85           | 0.825     | 0.517 | +1.53     | 76        | 2 / 25 (8%)     |
| 0.95           | 0.893     | 0.527 | +1.82     | 72        | 6 / 25 (24%)    |
| 0.99           | 0.940     | 0.521 | +2.08     | 76        | 22 / 25 (88%)   |
| 1.00           | 0.950     | 0.522 | +2.13     | 73        | 20 / 25 (80%)   |

Twenty-five replications per condition, two hundred permutations each. Raw
results are bundled at `data/power_analysis.json` (regenerated 2026-08-04;
see `README.md`'s "Last pipeline verification" for why that date matters).

**At the observed corpus size only near-absolute directional preference is
detectable.** Detection is zero up to a true prefix share of 0.75 and reaches
50% only near 0.99. Even a corpus in which *every* affix is a prefix is
detected in 20 of 25 replications rather than all 25 — one condition below
that, at p = 0.99, detection is actually slightly higher (22 of 25); at 25
replications per condition the two are within a binomial standard error of
each other, not a real reversal. The received figure of 59% prefixal
is detected **zero times in twenty-five**.

This settles the interpretation. The observed z = +0.23 is not evidence that
Linear A affixation is directionally balanced; it is a measurement with
essentially no power to distinguish any realistic degree of skew from none. The
method can distinguish a language in which every affix is a prefix from one in
which every affix is a suffix, and nothing in between.

This has a consequence that runs against an earlier draft of this analysis. The
received figure in the literature is 59% prefixal, contrasted with 12% in Linear
B and read as evidence that the language is agglutinative rather than inflecting.
It is tempting to report the measurement above as failing to reproduce that
figure and therefore challenging it. **That inference is invalid.** A ratio at
chance level cannot contradict anything; it is absence of directional evidence,
not evidence of the opposite direction. Note further that a chance baseline near
0.40 means the received 59%, measured this way, would sit *above* chance. The
result establishes only that this method has no directional power at this corpus
size, and the published figure stands unchallenged by it.

The same applies to language-family arguments. Prefix dominance has been used
against an Indo-European reading, since Indo-European morphology is
overwhelmingly suffixing. Having no directional evidence, this study withdraws
that argument in both directions: nothing here speaks for or against any family.
What survives is that affixation itself is real, which is compatible with
essentially every candidate and discriminates between none.

**A different question the same data could still answer.** Aggregate direction
is not measurable at this corpus size, but *which signs* function as affixes is
a distinct question, and two of the positional survivors from §5.4 are
candidates on distributional grounds alone: AB008, initial in 71 of 76
occurrences, and AB002, final in 64 of 70. Whether these edge-preferring signs
coincide with the affix relations found here is not tested here — it needs its
own null model, since edge-preference and affix-membership are not obviously
independent properties, and conflating them would risk manufacturing the
appearance of a finding from two measurements of related structure. Left for
future work rather than pursued in this paper.

### 5.6 The libation formula under two readings

Word division in the religious corpus is contested at five sites (D9), in the
genre where segmentation matters most. Rather than adjudicate, the segmentation
was run **twice, once under each reading**, and only structure surviving both is
reported.

Mean relative position proved the wrong estimator — on fragmentary texts a
missing head shifts every index, and it produced an ordering contradicting the
literature. Pairwise precedence is correct: for each pair of slots, how often does
one precede the other within a text.

**Both readings produce identical constraint sets: ten constraints, zero
contradicting pairs.** The derived order is

> ATAI-\*301-WA-JA → [SASARA slot] → U-NA-KA-NA-SI → I-PI-NA-MA → SI-RU-TE

recovered from distribution alone and subsequently found to agree with the
published schema. Independent recovery of an accepted result is evidence the
method works rather than a new finding; the useful corollary is that the ordering
is **invariant under the contested word division**.

The substantive result is qualified. In the SASARA slot the *existence* of an
alternation is boundary-stable, but its *form* is not: under one reading it is
presence against absence of an initial JA-, under the other it is JA- against A-.
These are different morphological analyses — an affix that may be absent, against
an affix with two allomorphs — and the corpus cannot decide between them. Any
claim about prefixation in the formula inherits this ambiguity and should state
which reading it assumes.

The whole formula rests on 119 word tokens under one reading and 124 under the
other, with individual slots attested two to eleven times. Nothing here should be
quoted without its n.

### 5.7 An arithmetic control

> **In brief.** One semantic claim in Linear A rests on arithmetic rather than
> language comparison, and it survives a permutation test at z = +13.4. This
> subsection was marked exploratory in an earlier draft; a proper null model
> shows that was too cautious.

One reading of a Linear A sign-group rests on internal evidence alone: KU-RO is
read as "total" because on balance-sheet tablets the numbers preceding it sum to
the number following. That is arithmetic, and holds whatever language the script
encodes.

**Sectioning.** Tablets are frequently mixed-commodity, carrying several totals,
each closing its own block. A section ends at a total and begins at whichever
comes last of: a horizontal ruling (the `ruling` token type recovered in §4.1); a
previous total; or a commodity logogram acting as a heading, distinguished from
an inline commodity by not being followed immediately by a numeral.

**The per-commodity rule makes no difference.** It was implemented on the
suggestion that it would raise the match rate, and it does not: with and without
it, the result is identical. Reported because a suggestion tested and found inert
is more useful than one silently dropped.

**Fractions.** Values graded `secure` or `derived` under the Corazza et al.
system are admitted; `tentative` and `unknown` are excluded. D13 is open, but the
matched cases are dominated by whole numbers and the result is unchanged either
way.

**Results**, over 26 testable KU-RO occurrences:

| outcome                                  | n  | share |
|------------------------------------------|----|-------|
| section sums exactly to the stated total | 10 | 38%   |
| off by exactly one unit                  | 3  | 12%   |
| mismatch                                 | 13 | 50%   |

**The off-by-one cases deserve separate mention.** HT 13 (131 against a stated
130), HT 94a (111 against 110) and HT 119 (159 against 160) miss by a single
unit. Scribal arithmetic error is a documented phenomenon in Linear B
accounting, and a single misread or miswritten numeral produces exactly this
signature. They are not counted as successes below.

**The null model is what makes this a test.** A raw match rate proves nothing,
because a section boundary chosen to make sums work is circular. Two nulls were
tried, and the first was wrong in an instructive way.

*The flawed null* resampled numerals from within the same section. When a section
covers most of a tablet, a random draw from it is nearly the section itself, so
the null was not independent of the observation. It returned an expected 8.4
exact matches of 26 — implying the observed 10 was barely above chance, which was
an artifact of the null rather than a fact about the data.

*The correct null* permutes which stated total is paired with which section, and
asks whether a section sums to *its own* total more often than to another
tablet's:

|                                | value       |
|--------------------------------|-------------|
| observed exact matches         | 10 of 26    |
| shuffled pairings, 2000 trials | 0.52 ± 0.71 |
| **z**                          | **+13.40**  |

**KI-RO, the contrast term**, is read as "deficit" and should *not* behave as a
running total. It matches in **0 of 7** cases. No other frequent two-sign group
reaches testable numbers.

**Reading.** The claim is supported strongly. What it establishes is that a
sign-group marks summation, not what word it represents or how it sounded; an
accountant's tally mark would test identically.

**The 50% mismatch rate is not a uniform residue.** Every numeral in this
corpus already carries a damage/completeness flag, recovered independently
of this test, so the mismatches can be checked against it directly rather
than left as an unweighted list of candidates. Of the 13 mismatches, **9
(69%) involve a numeral — in the summed section or in the stated total —
already flagged damaged or incomplete**; a misread or partly-preserved digit
is a plausible and directly evidenced explanation for those, not a guess.
The open D7 numeral conflicts, by contrast, do not apply here at all: none
of the four disputed sites (including `HT 10a`, which differs between
witnesses by an order of magnitude) appear among these 26 tablets. That
leaves **4 genuinely unexplained mismatches** — `HT25b`, `HT100`, `HT118`,
`HT123+124b` — where no numeral involved carries any damage flag, and the
section still does not sum. Sectioning this study cannot resolve remains a
live candidate for those four specifically; nothing here rules it out.

## 6. Adequacy: three bounded negatives

> **In brief.** The two standard routes to a structural fingerprint are closed
> for this corpus, and a third is closed at a stated sensitivity. These are not
> failures of method but absences of information, established by counting.

### 6.1 No alternation grid is recoverable

The Ventris method builds a syllabic grid from minimal pairs: attested types of
equal length differing at exactly one position, each asserting that two signs can
occupy the same slot in the same environment.

Raw counts look encouraging — 609 minimal pairs in the administrative corpus. But
they yield 514 distinct substitutions, meaning almost every sign pair alternates
exactly once, and the substitution graph is a single near-complete component of
96 of the 98 signs involved. A real paradigm has the opposite shape: a few pairs
alternating repeatedly, producing a sparse structured graph.

Against types resampled preserving length distribution and positional frequencies
(300 trials):

| metric                          | observed | null         | z         |
|---------------------------------|----------|--------------|-----------|
| minimal pairs                   | 609      | 683.7 ± 45.8 | **−1.63** |
| substitutions repeated          | 77       | 90.0 ± 15.7  | −0.83     |
| max repeats of one substitution | 4        | 4.4 ± 0.8    | −0.51     |

p(null ≥ observed) = **0.94** administrative, **0.95** religious. **The corpus
produces fewer minimal pairs than chance**, and every metric sits below the null
mean. With 519 short types over a 98-sign inventory, apparent minimal pairs are
collisions. No better algorithm recovers a grid; the information is absent.

### 6.2 Conditional entropy is not estimable

H2, the entropy of a sign given its predecessor, is the measure that actually
discriminates script types. It cannot be computed here, for arithmetic reasons.

| corpus         | bigram tokens | distinct observed | possible (k²) | coverage |
|----------------|---------------|-------------------|---------------|----------|
| whole          | 1875          | 929               | 73984         | **1.3%** |
| administrative | 1386          | 729               | 68644         | 1.1%     |
| religious      | 489           | 312               | 8649          | 3.6%     |

There are 0.025 observations per cell of the bigram table, and 63% of observed
bigrams occur exactly once. A conventional five-per-cell requirement would need
roughly 370,000 within-word bigram tokens against the 1875 available — short by
more than two orders of magnitude. **Any H2 figure from this corpus measures the
sampling, not the script.**

**Could sparse-data methods recover it?** Good-Turing and Bayesian smoothing are
designed for exactly this situation, so the objection deserves a test rather than
an assertion. Five estimators were applied to the observed table and, as a
control, to synthetic corpora generated from a *known* Markov chain at the same
sample size.

On the observed corpus the estimators span **3.68 bits (maximum likelihood) to
7.02 bits (Laplace)**, against a theoretical maximum of 7.12. That range includes
both "highly structured" and "very nearly random", which is already the answer:
the estimate is a property of the prior, not of the data.

The control explains why, and the failure is not simple imprecision:

| true H2                   | MLE         | Laplace      | Bayes α=0.5  | Good-Turing |
|---------------------------|-------------|--------------|--------------|-------------|
| 1.31 (concentrated chain) | 1.16 (−12%) | 6.86 (+422%) | 6.62 (+404%) | 1.49 (+13%) |
| 5.84 (diffuse chain)      | 3.54 (−39%) | 7.07 (+21%)  | 6.97 (+19%)  | 6.92 (+19%) |

**The estimators are biased in opposite directions, and which is right depends on
the answer being sought.** Maximum likelihood tracks a concentrated distribution
acceptably and underestimates a diffuse one badly, because unseen cells get zero.
The smoothers do the reverse, inflating toward the uniform prior regardless of
the truth.

No sparse-data method resolves this, because choosing between them is choosing a
prior rather than inferring from data. The adequacy result stands.

### 6.3 No phonological signal in the inherited values

If inherited Linear B values carry Minoan phonological information, applying them
should expose dependence between the vowels of consecutive signs. Vowels rather
than full CV values, because five vowels give a 25-cell table this corpus can
fill.

Only 21.2% of sign tokens carry a value the standard reference calls certain;
another 7.3% are "possible"; 52.0% are assumed by shape analogy and never
independently verified; 19.6% have no value at all. The test was therefore run at
each confidence level.

| level    | bigrams | per cell | observed MI | null            | z     | p            |
|----------|---------|----------|-------------|-----------------|-------|--------------|
| certain  | 62      | 2.5      | —           | —               | —     | underpowered |
| possible | 126     | 5.0      | 0.2322      | 0.2144 ± 0.0637 | +0.28 | 0.37         |
| assumed  | 1217    | 48.7     | 0.0417      | 0.0288 ± 0.0097 | +1.33 | 0.10         |

No detectable signal. The weak positive trend at the assumed level is not
evidence.

**Re-specification.** The standard view is that Minoan may have had a three-vowel
system, since in Linear B most signs carrying /o/ and one carrying /e/ are Greek
innovations rather than inherited. On that view the test above is mis-specified.
Re-run with vowels collapsed e→i and o→u, sampling improves from 49 to 135
observations per cell and the weak trend **disappears entirely**, going slightly
negative (z = −0.34, p = 0.56). The conclusion is unchanged and now rests on a
better-specified test.

**Power.** A null is uninformative without knowing what could have been detected.
Injecting synthetic vowel harmony into words of the observed shape and length
distribution: harmony of strength 0.10 is detected **100% of the time**, 0.05
about half. None is observed.

**Strong vowel harmony is therefore excluded.** If the inherited values are
approximately right, the language behind Linear A did not have Turkic- or
Uralic-style harmony. This must not be overread: many agglutinative languages
lack harmony entirely — Japanese, Swahili, Quechua, Georgian — so what is excluded
is a specific areal-typological feature, **not** agglutination as a morphological
type.

### 6.4 Methods precluded by corpus adequacy

A common response to undeciphered scripts is to propose increasingly
sophisticated computational methods. The argument of this section is that
method complexity cannot compensate for insufficient information: before a
model can be expected to recover linguistic structure, the corpus must
contain enough independent observations to identify the parameters the
model attempts to estimate.

§6.1–6.3 show that condition is not met here. Minimal-pair recovery
performs below chance, conditional entropy cannot be estimated within
acceptable confidence bounds, and lexical recurrence is insufficient for
stable estimation of higher-order statistical structure. Consequently,
several classes of computational linguistic method are presently
underdetermined by the available evidence, not merely untried:

| Method                                            | Supported by this corpus? | Reason                                                                                                                                                                      |
|---------------------------------------------------|---------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Morphological segmentation (Morfessor, BPE, etc.) | No                        | Requires substantially greater lexical recurrence than the corpus contains; segmentation becomes driven by optimisation assumptions, not recoverable morphology.            |
| Hidden Markov Models for morpheme classes         | No                        | Transition and emission probabilities cannot be estimated robustly; multiple incompatible models fit the data equally well.                                                 |
| Bayesian cognate scoring                          | No                        | Requires established lexical correspondences or reliable semantic anchors; neither exists for a corpus this size, undeciphered.                                             |
| Automatic sound-correspondence discovery          | No                        | Depends on aligned lexical sets whose existence cannot be demonstrated independently.                                                                                       |
| Syllable inventory optimisation                   | No                        | Several competing inventories satisfy the data equally well; the corpus cannot discriminate among them.                                                                     |
| Affix alignment                                   | Limited                   | Recurring terminal sequences are detectable (§5.5's affixation result), but grammatical interpretation, morpheme boundaries, and function remain statistically unsupported. |
| Constraint-based phonotactic modelling            | No                        | Entropy estimation itself fails adequacy testing (§6.2); higher-order phonotactic constraints cannot be estimated reliably as a result.                                     |
| Cross-entropy ranking of candidate languages      | No                        | Rankings would primarily reflect corpus sparsity and preprocessing assumptions, not genuine linguistic affinity.                                                            |
| Statistical translation lattices                  | No                        | Translation models require semantic correspondences unavailable for an undeciphered corpus.                                                                                 |
| Automatic proto-language reconstruction           | No                        | Requires established cognate sets and regular sound correspondences, neither presently inferable.                                                                           |

None of this invalidates the methods themselves; it marks the limits the
available evidence imposes. The obstacle is informational, not algorithmic
— greater model sophistication cannot recover information the corpus does
not contain.

Methods operating on shallow structural regularities remain the exception,
and remain appropriate: this study's own permutation-tested affixation
(§5.5), the KU-RO arithmetic null model (§5.7), corpus auditing (§4), and
adequacy testing itself (§6.1–6.3) are all statistically identifiable
despite the sparsity that rules out the methods above. The methodological
response to an information-limited corpus is not greater model complexity
but explicit identification of which questions the available evidence can,
and cannot, answer.

## 7. The language question

> **In brief.** This study takes no position on the affiliation of Minoan,
> because the measures that could bear on it are demonstrably beyond this corpus.
> That is itself a result, and it constrains what any study of comparable size can
> honestly claim.

Proposals for Minoan affiliation are numerous and none commands assent. Rather
than add another, this study asked a prior question: which discriminating
measures can this corpus actually support?

The answer is discouraging and worth stating precisely.

**Sign-distributional grid recovery** — the Ventris route, and the only method
that has ever succeeded on an Aegean script — is closed. Minimal pairs fall below
chance (§6.1). The route is unavailable not because it has been tried and failed
but because the corpus contains no paradigmatic signal to extract.

**Script-typological classification via conditional entropy** is closed by
counting, short of the requirement by two orders of magnitude (§6.2).

**Phonotactic inference from inherited values** is closed at a stated
sensitivity: vowel-sequencing dependency is undetectable, and harmony of strength
0.10 or above is excluded (§6.3).

**Morphological typology** is available in one direction only. Affixation is real
(z ≈ 6). Its direction is not measurable (z ≈ +0.25), which removes the one
morphological statistic that has been used in affiliation arguments (§5.5).

What remains — sign inventory, register differences, word length, formula
structure — is compatible with essentially every candidate language and
discriminates between none.

A note on the genetic evidence, which is sometimes invoked. Lazaridis et al.
(2017) found Minoans and Mycenaeans genetically similar, both drawing at least
three-quarters of their ancestry from the first Neolithic farmers of western
Anatolia and the Aegean, but also found that the Mycenaeans carried additional
ancestry related to Eastern European and Siberian hunter-gatherers which the
Minoans lack. Their own framing is *continuity but not isolation*. Two cautions
follow. The additional Mycenaean component is the one conventionally associated
with Indo-European arrival, so the genetics does not support an unbroken
population premise. And more fundamentally, genetic continuity cannot license
linguistic inference at all: language replacement without population replacement
is common, and the discontinuity at issue here — Linear B writes Greek, Linear A
does not — is a cross-linguistic script adaptation, not internal drift.

The honest position is that this corpus cannot settle the affiliation question,
and that a study of this size which claims to has not checked whether its
statistics are computable.

## 8. Replicating Packard (1974)

> **In brief.** Packard's statistical defense of transferring Linear B sound
> values to Linear A has not been re-run in fifty-two years. Re-running it under
> his own rules, with four thousand permutations against his nine, reproduces his
> weak evidence and independently confirms his strong evidence. His conclusion
> stands. The figure the field cites is the weaker half of his argument.

### 8.1 Why this matters

Every phonetic edition of Linear A — and therefore every study that reads a
Linear A word aloud — rests on the assumption that signs sharing a shape with
Linear B share its sound value. The statistical warrant for that assumption is
Packard's, refined by Pope and Raison, and circulates as a ratio "just over
2 : 1" in favor of the Linear B values over nine fictitious controls.

Nine controls cannot estimate a null distribution, compute a standard deviation,
or produce a p-value. That was a reasonable limit in 1974 and is not one now.

### 8.2 Getting the method right

This replication was wrong three times before it was right, and the corrections
are instructive about working from secondary description.

**Version 1** operationalized "confirmatory alternation" from a summary of
Packard's method, since the primary source was not yet in hand. It used
equal-length minimal pairs scored as sharing a consonant *or* a vowel.

**Version 2**, after obtaining the relevant page, found the pairing rule was
different: three positional categories, of which one explicitly admits unequal
lengths (three signs against four). Version 1 had never generated that class and
had generated many pairs the scheme does not admit.

**Version 3**, after obtaining the summary tables, found the categories are
further split by how many signs are shared, and that Packard assigns these
classes weights of 2, 2 and **zero**. Versions 1 and 2 had tested only the
classes he discounts.

**Version 4**, after obtaining the full chapter, found the scoring rule stated
verbatim: alternations count as significant if the alternating signs have the
**same consonant** — not same consonant or same vowel. The null construction was
also recovered: signs ordered by descending frequency, divided into groups of
ten, values rotated within each group. His nine alternatives are therefore
**exhaustive rotations** of that scheme, which is a better defense of n = 9 than
this study initially credited him with.

### 8.3 Result: internal alternations

Under Packard's own rules, with his categories and his same-consonant criterion:

| position  | our groups | our confirmatory | his groups | his confirmatory |
|-----------|------------|------------------|------------|------------------|
| final     | 38         | 4                | 39         | 10               |
| medial    | 22         | 2                | 26         | 4                |
| initial   | 32         | 1                | 42         | 5                |
| **total** | **92**     | **7**            | **107**    | **19**           |

Group counts track his closely (92 against 107), confirming the pairing rule is
now correct. Confirmatory rate is lower: 0.101 against his 0.178.

| test                               | result                                         |
|------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------|
| his 1/12 chance model, on our data | 7 observed vs 5.8 expected = **1.22 : 1**      |
| his nine rotations, on our corpus  | mean 4.44, observed 7 = **1.57 : 1**           |
| 4000 banded permutations           | 4.51 ± 2.10, **z = +1.19, p = 0.17**, 1.55 : 1 |

Against his 2.1 : 1. **Direction replicates; magnitude does not, and the effect
does not reach significance.**

Packard says as much himself. Having reported that seven of nine decipherments
produced at least half as many plausible alternations as the true values, he
concludes that a significant number of the Linear B alternations must be accepted
as fortuitous. Five random decipherments matched the true values on initial
alternations, which is why he weights that class zero.

### 8.4 Result: the Knossos name parallels

Packard states that the name parallels are what "demonstrates conclusively that
at least some of the Linear B phonetic values are valid for Linear A." That is a
different test, and it is the load-bearing one.

His criterion: both sign-groups complete and longer than two signs; the first two
signs identical plus the consonant of the third; final vowel or an obvious Linear
B suffix disregarded, since a non-Greek name might be accommodated to a Greek
inflectional pattern.

Applied here against an independent Linear B lexicon (429 names of three or more
signs; 449 Linear A types of three or more signs), of which 154 are attested at
Knossos specifically — checked against a Knossos find-site word export from
DĀMOS, Database of Mycenaean at Oslo (`data/README.md` item 5b) — which is the
criterion Packard actually specifies:

|                                  | true values | his nine rotations            | ratio        | 2000 permutations | z         | p          |
|----------------------------------|-------------|-------------------------------|--------------|-------------------|-----------|------------|
| **LB names, Knossos-restricted** | **10**      | mean 2.11, **0 of 9 beat it** | **4.74 : 1** | 2.25 ± 1.79       | **+4.34** | **0.0020** |
| LB names only (unrestricted)     | 20          | mean 4.33, 0 of 9 beat it     | 4.62 : 1     | 5.01 ± 2.72       | +5.51     | 0.0005     |
| LB full lexicon                  | 31          | mean 7.67, 0 of 9 beat it     | 4.04 : 1     | 8.15 ± 3.70       | +6.18     | 0.0005     |

Packard's own Table 14 gives 4.3 for the Knossos name class, and he reports
fifteen matches against about three random, a ratio near 5 : 1. **On the
criterion that actually matches his, this study obtains 10 matches against a
mean of 2.11 random, 4.74 : 1** — an independent replication, on a different
corpus, with four thousand times his permutation count, fifty-two years later,
and the first time the absolute count (10 against his 15) has been directly
comparable rather than inflated by a looser name list.

### 8.5 What this establishes

Two findings pointing in opposite directions, and both are his.

The internal alternation evidence is weak, and Packard says so, weighting the
classes with adequate counts at 2, 2 and 0. The name-parallel evidence is strong,
and it replicates at z = +4.34 on the Knossos-restricted criterion (z = +5.51
unrestricted).

**Packard's actual conclusion is therefore supported: at least some of the Linear
B phonetic values are valid for Linear A.** He never claimed more.

Two consequences.

First, the transfer of sound values has better statistical support than the
theoretical objections alone would suggest. The values carry real information —
demonstrably, at p = 0.0005 — even though the language is not Greek. This bears
on §6.3: the vowel-harmony null there should be read as evidence that *Minoan
lacked strong harmony*, not as evidence that the transcription is wrong, because
the transcription is now independently supported.

Second, and this is the critical finding of the section: **the figure the field
cites is the weaker half.** The "2 : 1" that circulates as the warrant for
backward projection is the alternation figure — the half Packard discounts, on
counts of 10, 4 and 5, one class of which he weights at zero. The name-parallel
figure of roughly 4–5 : 1 is the half that carries the argument and the one that
deserves citation.

### 8.6 Caveats

The name-parallel criterion is now restricted to Knossos-attested names, as
Packard required (checked against DĀMOS; §8.4 above), and the unrestricted
429-name figure is retained only for comparison with earlier drafts of this
work. What this does not resolve: this study's digital corpus is not Packard's
own `Corpus transnuméré`, so the underlying word-attestation data is not
identical to his even where the naming criterion now matches.

Packard works in Raison-Pope sign numbers with his own value table; this study
works in GORILA numbering. Sign-level correspondence with his experiment is
therefore not guaranteed, though the close agreement in group counts
(92 against 107) suggests it is substantially right.

His Appendix D, containing the nine value assignments in full, has not been
consulted; the rotations used here are reconstructed from his description of the
construction method.

## 9. Four artifacts: a note on method

> **In brief.** Four times in this project a defect in the comparison tooling
> produced what looked like a substantive finding. One was statistically
> significant, mechanistically plausible and false. This section reports them
> because they are the strongest argument for the discipline the paper proposes.

Automated collation between editions of an undeciphered script is unusually
error-prone, because the editions differ in *notation* far more than they differ
in *reading*, and notation differences are silent. Four instances arose here.

**1. A scare-quoted column header.** One witness prints its fraction column
header with quotation marks — `"fraction"` — expressing editorial reservation
about the values. A key lookup on the header string silently dropped the entire
column, manufacturing roughly 89 phantom mismatches. Raw agreement read 70.5%
before this and related fixes; the true figure is 92.0%.

**2. Malformed HTML nesting.** In the scraped free-text records, red-font
commentary tags sometimes *wrap* black-font transliteration rather than
containing only commentary. Removing red subtrees naively deleted 1002 real
records, leaving 190. The same pass fragmented sign groups on whitespace inside
underline tags, suppressing every doubtful-reading flag: 0 reported where there
are 121.

**3. Vessel notation.** One corpus labels a vessel sign `*815` where its GORILA
identifier is A402-VAS, writes the VAS suffix as `-VS`, and omits the ligature
marker the other uses. This produced 20 false divergences.

**4. Number-versus-name conventions.** In the long-tail triage, 32 of 84 sites
resolved to the same sign under two naming systems — HIDE and \*180, MU and \*23,
SO and \*363 — with a further 9 arising from ligature tokenization or annotation
markers.

Artifact 3 is the instructive one. It produced a finding: one scribe showed
divergences at nine times the baseline rate, p = 9.8 × 10⁻⁸, surviving
Benjamini-Hochberg correction across 22 hands. It was mechanistically plausible —
a scribe with idiosyncratic letterforms is exactly what one would expect to
generate contested readings. It was also entirely false. The scribe in question
wrote two vessel-inventory tablets, and every divergence was vessel notation; the
underlying sign identifiers agreed throughout. After the fix, no hand is
significant.

**Significance testing gives no protection against a systematic artifact, because
the artifact is systematic.** A convention difference that applies to every
instance of a sign class will concentrate wherever that class concentrates, and
will therefore produce clustering, and clustering will produce small p-values.

The working rule adopted, and recommended: **a positive result from automated
collation is provisional until the specific documents driving it have been
inspected by hand.** Negative results are safer, which is one reason the adequacy
findings of §6 can be trusted more than the scribal result could have been.

A corollary for reporting: the first divergence figure a comparator produces
measures the comparator at least as much as the corpus. Every agreement figure in
§4.5 is the result of several rounds of convention folding, and each round was
driven by inspecting the residual rather than by assuming it was real.

## 10. Transferability

> **In brief:** three of the four components are script-agnostic; the adequacy
> protocol in particular applies unchanged to any undeciphered corpus, and the
> counting arguments that closed two routes here will close or open them
> elsewhere by the same arithmetic.

The framework was developed on Linear A, but only one component is specific to it.

**Script-agnostic.** The governing principle — sign identity as data, labels as
lossy derivatives — applies to any corpus with a standard sign inventory and
competing transliteration conventions, which is to say every undeciphered script
with more than one edition. Structured preservation of damage and reading
certainty applies wherever texts are fragmentary. The null-model discipline
applies to any distributional claim.

Most transferable is the **adequacy protocol**. The bigram-cell counting argument
that closed conditional entropy here (§6.2) works identically on Cypro-Minoan,
Proto-Elamite, the Indus corpus and Rongorongo: count the inventory, square it,
count the observed bigrams, divide. So does the minimal-pair null that closed
grid recovery (§6.1), and so does the requirement that a negative result be
accompanied by a power calibration.

That last point generalizes furthest. Undeciphered-script studies report negative
results frequently and power calibrations almost never, which makes those
negatives uninterpretable: a null with unknown sensitivity excludes nothing.

**Adaptable but not portable.** The convention-folding comparator is
edition-specific by nature. Its *design* transfers — fold documented equivalences
before counting, inspect the residual by hand, iterate — but its alias tables do
not.

Candidate next targets, in rough order of fit: **Cypro-Minoan**, **the Indus
script** (~4000 inscriptions, ~400 signs, multiple digital editions with
different sign inventories), **Proto-Elamite** (~1600 tablets, largely
numerical, digital corpora in progress), and **Rongorongo** (~25 objects, ~800
glyphs, where structured preservation of uncertainty matters most because the
corpus is smallest).

**Linear B** is the instructive case. The framework works on deciphered scripts
too, and applying it there would establish what editorial decisions are embedded
in a corpus whose language is known — a check unavailable for Linear A, and
therefore a way of validating the method itself.

**Cypro-Minoan** is the obvious next target: related, similarly small, similarly
subject to over-claiming, and with a live dispute about whether it constitutes one
script or several that is precisely a distributional question. The adequacy
protocol would establish before any analysis whether that dispute is decidable
from the extant corpus, which on present sizes seems doubtful and would be worth
demonstrating rather than assuming.

### From method to software

The preceding discussion concerns the method. The repository is also, directly,
software, and it is worth being precise about what that means in practice for
anyone who wants to point it at a different corpus rather than only read about
the approach.

**It is not plug-and-play, and no part of it claims to be.** There is no single
command that takes an arbitrary undeciphered corpus and returns an adequacy
report. What exists is a worked implementation of the method above, with Linear A
as the running example throughout every stage — the adequacy protocol, the
null-model machinery, the divergence-register discipline, and the power-analysis
technique are implemented in a form that does not know anything about Linear A
specifically, while the data loader, the canonical sign inventory, and the
edition-alias tables are Linear A's own and do not transfer as data, only as
pattern.

| component                                 | transfers as code | requires rebuilding                              |
|-------------------------------------------|-------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| adequacy protocol (observations-per-cell) | yes               | —                                                |
| null models (resampling, permutation)     | yes               | —                                                |
| divergence-register schema and discipline | yes               | contents are new                                 |
| power-analysis method                     | yes               | —                                                |
| entropy / conditional-entropy estimators  | yes               | —                                                |
| data loader                               | no                | corpus-specific file format                      |
| canonical sign inventory                  | no                | script-specific                                  |
| edition/convention alias tables           | pattern only      | populated with the new editions' own conventions |

Adapting the software to a different corpus is in practice a three-step
data-engineering task rather than a re-derivation of the statistics: write a
loader that turns the target corpus's actual export format into the same
working-record shape used throughout this pipeline (one record per document, one
typed and damage-flagged token list per record); define a canonical sign
identifier for the target script and the alias tables that fold each edition's
naming convention onto it, for the same reason argued in §12.2 for Linear A —
comparing on the printed label rather than the identifier is comparing editorial
convention, not the script; and rerun the adequacy protocol, null models,
divergence register and power analysis unchanged against the new loader's output.
Cross-witness comparison, where multiple digital editions exist, needs one parser
per witness export format but reuses the comparison logic as written.

None of this removes the need for domain judgment about where the adequacy bar
sits for a given inventory size, which alternations are meaningful to test, or
which witnesses are worth comparing — that judgment does not transfer, and
nothing here claims it does.

## 11. Implications for Aegean studies

> **In brief.** One correction to shared infrastructure, one recovered dataset,
> one replication that redirects a citation, and a set of stated bounds on what
> corpora of this size can support.

**A correction to shared infrastructure.** A digital corpus in public use
systematically inverts sheep and goats across fourteen documents. Any
quantitative work on livestock, commodity ratios or personnel-to-animal
allocations drawn from that corpus carries the error. The correction is small in
tokens (57) and consequential in kind: it is invisible to a user of the corpus
and undetectable without an independent witness.

**A method for recovering a witness.** SigLA's corpus was accessible in principle
and unreadable in practice, being stored in a binary format that discards field
names. The decoder released with this paper makes 802 documents and 5144
attestations, with certainty and boundary annotation, available for statistical
work for the first time. That has value independent of anything argued here.

**A redirected citation.** Packard's 2 : 1 is the alternation figure — the half
of his argument he weights at 2, 2 and 0, and which does not reach significance
when re-run with adequate permutations. The name-parallel figure of roughly
4–5 : 1 is the half that carries his conclusion and replicates at p = 0.0005.
Work depending on the validity of Linear B sound values in Linear A should cite
the latter.

**Stated bounds.** The three adequacy results are the least glamorous
contribution and possibly the most useful. Grid recovery, conditional entropy and
vowel-phonotactic inference are all routinely attempted on Linear A, and the
adequacy check is almost never shown. This paper shows it, with the thresholds
quantified, so that future attempts can be assessed against a number rather than
against optimism.

**A methodological caution.** The four artifacts of §9 arose in a project that was
actively looking for them. Studies that compare editions without folding
conventions first, or that report a first divergence count as a finding, will
produce inflated disagreement figures. The 137-to-9 triage suggests the inflation
factor can approach an order of magnitude.

**What this does for decipherment, stated briefly.** This work does not advance
decipherment directly. It provides a corrected corpus, quantitative bounds on
what that corpus can support, and a reproducible framework for evaluating future
claims. Three of the requirements a decipherment would need — a reliable corpus,
statistical support for the phonetic values, and structural constraints any
proposal must explain — are addressed here; language identification, grammatical
analysis, independently verified sound values, a bilingual text and identified
loanwords are not, and the last of these does not exist.

**What is not implied.** Nothing here advances decipherment, and §7 argues that
this corpus cannot. Nothing here bears on the affiliation of Minoan. Nothing here
overturns Packard, Younger, Douros or Duhoux; where their work is examined it is
substantially vindicated, and where an earlier draft of this study thought
otherwise, §5.5 records the retraction.

## 12. Limitations and open items

> **In brief.** The corpus base is thin, one witness is unreviewed, several
> comparisons are looser than their originals, and ten defects remain open with
> named remedies. This section states all of it.

### 12.1 The binding constraint

Complete multi-sign sign-groups — the base for all morphological work — number
**927 instances**, of which 519 are distinct administrative types and 142
religious. Roughly a quarter of the corpus is fragmentary. Individual libation
formula slots are attested two to eleven times.

Nothing in this paper should be quoted without its n, and any finding resting on
fewer than about fifteen attestations should be treated as a hypothesis.

### 12.2 Provenance

The working corpus derives from an unreviewed extraction of a non-peer-reviewed
tabulation of a peer-reviewed edition. Three sweeps at 92.0%, 94.7% and 95.9%
against independent witnesses substantially answer the obvious objection, but do
not eliminate it. A reviewer is entitled to ask whether findings about this
corpus are findings about Linear A, and the honest answer is that they are
findings about the best digital approximation currently available.

### 12.3 Looser criteria than the originals

The Packard name-parallel replication now restricts its Linear B lexicon to
Knossos-attested names (154 of 429, checked against DĀMOS), matching his
criterion; the underlying corpus is still not his own, so the restriction closes
the naming gap without closing the corpus gap. The alternation replication uses
GORILA numbering against his Raison-Pope numbering. The SigLA comparison is at
sign-family level, since SigLA does not record gender variants. Each remaining
looseness applies equally across compared conditions, so ratios hold; absolute
counts do not fully transfer.

### 12.4 Not attempted

Duhoux's affix analysis has not been examined in the original, being unavailable
digitally. Given that this study's reconstruction of Packard's method was wrong
three times before the primary source corrected it, no weight should be placed on
this study's reading of Duhoux's method, and §5.5 places none.

Davis's treatment of the libation corpus and Steele and Meißner on backward
projection have likewise not been consulted in the original.

Per-commodity sectioning for KU-RO was implemented and tested (§5.7); it
makes no difference to the match rate, so it does not explain the 38%.
Beyond the damage-flag check already applied there, no further sectioning
rule is currently proposed or tested for the four mismatches damage doesn't
explain — that remains genuinely not attempted.

### 12.5 D4 is adjudicated at family level only

SigLA does not record the M/F gender variants our source carries. The
adjudication of §4.2 therefore establishes that the source assigns AB21 where
AB22 belongs and vice versa, but says nothing about whether the gender assignment
within each sign is correct.

Concretely: the corpus now reads `AB022F` where it previously read `AB021F`. The
`AB022` is confirmed at fifteen of fifteen sites. The `F` is confirmed by
nothing.

Could the family-level mapping conceal a different error? In principle yes. If
the source's gender variants were also mis-assigned, family-level agreement with
the third witness would be unaffected and the error would persist undetected.
Nothing in this study tests that, and any analysis distinguishing male from
female livestock should treat the variant as unverified pending plate collation
of the fourteen documents.

### 12.6 Open register items

| id     | issue                                          | remedy                      |
|--------|------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------|
| D7     | 4 numeral conflicts, one an order of magnitude | GORILA plate collation      |
| D8/D10 | 9 genuine sign conflicts after triage          | GORILA plate collation      |
| D9     | 4 word-division sites (1 of 5 explained)       | GORILA plates; not in SigLA |
| D11    | AP Za 2, three editors against the source      | GORILA vol. 4 plate         |
| D13    | fraction K: 1/16 against 1/10                  | specialist judgement        |

All are documented in the machine-readable register with the evidence that would
settle each. None blocks the results reported here: D7 gates accounting
reconstruction, D9 gates formula segmentation, D13 gates metrology, and the
structural and replication results depend on none of them.

### 12.7 A note on the corrections in this paper

Five substantive claims made during this project were withdrawn or reversed
before publication: the affix-direction challenge to Duhoux (§5.5), the scribal
clustering effect (§9), the A301 positional finding (§5.4), the initial reading
of the Packard replication as a refutation (§8.2), and the hypothesis that
disagreements concentrate in doubtful readings (§4.5).

They are reported rather than removed. In a field where negative and null results
are structurally under-reported, a paper that shows only its surviving claims
gives a misleading impression of how reliably such claims are generated.

Each failed differently, and the differences are the useful part.

**The affix-direction challenge** failed because a measurement was reported
without its null. The observed 42% prefix share looked like a finding until the
chance baseline turned out to be 40%. *Lesson: a proportion is not a result until
the proportion expected by chance is known.* The power analysis of §5.5 went
further and showed the measure cannot detect a 59% skew at all, so the original
claim was not merely unsupported but untestable.

**The scribal clustering effect** failed because a systematic artifact produced
genuine statistical significance. It reached p = 9.8 × 10⁻⁸ and survived
multiple-comparison correction. *Lesson: significance testing does not protect
against systematic error, because the error is systematic. Inspect the driving
documents.*

**The A301 positional finding** failed because a pattern was read as meaningful
without asking what the pattern would look like by chance. All fifteen
occurrences medial is striking until one notices that in words of that length,
medial-only is unremarkable. *Lesson: intuitions about what is surprising are
unreliable when the alternative has not been computed.*

**The Packard replication as refutation** failed because a method was
reconstructed from a secondary description. Three successive versions were wrong
in different ways, each corrected only by the primary source, and the direction
of every correction was toward the original author being more careful than his
citations. *Lesson: do not replicate from a summary. If the primary source is
unavailable, say what has not been checked.*

**The doubtful-readings hypothesis** failed because a single vivid case was
generalized. The KN Zc 6 finding — an unreadable sign exactly at a contested
boundary — was compelling and turned out not to describe the corpus: restricting
to confident readings makes agreement slightly worse. *Lesson: a mechanism that
explains one case is a hypothesis about the rest, not a finding about it.*

The common thread is that four of the five failed by omitting a comparison — a
null, a chance baseline, a primary source, a corpus-wide check — rather than by
computing anything incorrectly. The arithmetic was right every time.

## 13. What this corpus can and cannot support

> **In brief.** This section exists to be consulted before starting a project,
> not just read once. Every closure below is arithmetic, not opinion, and is
> traceable to a specific count in a specific section — check the count, not
> the conclusion, if a research direction depends on it. The purpose is
> practical: several of the closures below are exactly the premise of
> projects that take years to build, most obviously machine-learning
> pipelines trained toward phonetic or comparative conclusions. If the
> corpus cannot support the premise, no amount of model sophistication
> changes that, and the project's outcome is knowable in advance.

### 13.1 Supported

| Can be done                                                                                         | Evidence                                                                                                        | Section  |

| Score cognates, discover sound correspondences, or reconstruct a proto-language automatically | All require aligned lexical sets or established correspondences; none exist for an undeciphered corpus this size                                                      | §6.4     |
| Rank candidate source languages by statistical fit                                            | A ranking would reflect corpus sparsity and preprocessing choices, not linguistic affinity                                                                            | §6.4     |
| Settle, or make progress toward settling, the affiliation of the language                     | Every measure that could discriminate between candidates is one of the closures above; what remains is compatible with essentially every candidate and rules out none | §7       |

### 13.3 A specific note on machine-learning and LLM-based approaches

None of the above is specific to classical statistical methods. A large
language model, however capable, cannot estimate a conditional entropy from
1,875 tokens more reliably than the five estimators tested in §6.2 — the
limit is the data, not the estimator, and §6.2 already shows every
estimator disagreeing with every other by hundreds of percent at this
sample size. Nor can an LLM recover morpheme boundaries the corpus lacks
the recurrence to support (§6.4), or discover sound correspondences with no
aligned lexical set to anchor them (§6.4).

There is a second, LLM-specific risk beyond running out of signal: a model
pretrained on the decipherment and Aegean-studies literature can produce
fluent, plausible-sounding Greek, Anatolian, or Semitic readings that
reflect its training data's discussion of Linear A, not any pattern in the
corpus itself. Because the failure mode is fluency rather than an error
message, it is harder to notice than a classical method's underpowered
null — the output looks like a finding. Any such system should be run
through the same adequacy checks as §6, before results are read, and its
outputs checked against §6.1–6.2's counting arguments specifically: an
information-theoretic claim implies a bigram-count requirement, computable
in one line, regardless of what produced the claim.

### 13.4 What would change this

Every closure above is a statement about *this corpus at its current size*,
not a permanent fact about Linear A. §6.2's requirement is arithmetic: an
order-of-magnitude increase in deciphered or newly-excavated text narrows
the entropy gap; two orders of magnitude closes it. §6.1's minimal-pair
null would need to invert, not just improve, which a larger corpus could
in principle do if the underlying sign system has paradigmatic structure
at all. Until then, the honest position is not "impossible" but "not
supported by the evidence that exists" — which is a narrower, checkable
claim, and the one this paper defends.

## Appendix C: Tests

The repository carries a regression suite in three groups: licensing hygiene
(asserting that no third-party corpus data is committed), structural invariants
(schema conformance, consistency between damage flags and the derived `complete`
field, presence of a confidence grade on every measure value, and application of
the D4 correction), and published figures (the numerical claims of this paper
expressed as assertions).

A failure in the third group is informative rather than defective: it indicates
that the upstream corpus has changed and that the figures reported here no
longer describe the data. The suite therefore verifies the corpus checksum first
and skips the dependent assertions when it differs.

This is not a hypothetical safeguard. During preparation the suite caught a
stale figure — the phantom-type inflation of §4.1, reported as 383 before the D4
correction and the adoption of the Corazza fraction system, and 374 on the
corrected corpus. The corrected value is the one reported.

## Appendix D: Decoding the SigLA database

> Released as a standalone tool: `src/ocaml_marshal.py` in the repository has no
> dependency on the rest of the pipeline and can decode any OCaml `Marshal`
> stream.

### A note on access and license

SigLA's corpus is served as a publicly accessible JavaScript file,
`database.js`, at `https://sigla.phis.me/`. The decoder released with this paper
downloads that file and parses its OCaml `Marshal` format. The data is used
under its stated **CC BY-NC-SA 4.0** license: attributed throughout,
non-commercially, and with any derivative carrying the same terms.

No authentication, paywall or technical protection measure is involved, and none
was circumvented. The license affirmatively grants the rights exercised here.

Correspondence with the SigLA authors is outstanding as a courtesy, and to
establish a documented route for others and for formal attribution. It is not a
precondition of the use made: the legal basis is the public file's explicit
license terms.

Two things follow for reproducibility. The repository does not redistribute
SigLA data, so a reader wishing to check the SigLA-dependent results retrieves
it from the original source, at its current version, under the license — which
is a better arrangement than working from a redistributed snapshot. And the
decoder is released because the format documentation has independent value:
`Marshal` is OCaml's standard serialization and the implementation is general.

### Why the data was unreadable

SigLA is written in OCaml and compiled to JavaScript. Its corpus ships in
`database.js` as two OCaml `Marshal` blobs, each escaped as decimal octets inside
a JavaScript string literal — the file begins `"\132\149\166\190...`, which is
the marshal magic number `0x8495A6BE` rendered in decimal.

The decisive obstacle is not the escaping but the format. **`Marshal` stores
records positionally.** A record with fields `{sign; unsure; meta}` is written as
a block of three values in declaration order, with no field names anywhere in the
stream. Searching the file for `unsure` returns nothing, and would return nothing
even if every attestation carried the field, which every attestation does.

This is not obfuscation. `Marshal` is OCaml's standard serialization for
same-program round-tripping, where the type is known at both ends. It is simply
not designed to be read by anything else.

### What was implemented

The format is documented in the OCaml runtime sources (`intext.h`, `extern.c`).
The decoder implements:

- the 20-byte header: magic, data length, object count, two size fields;
- the prefix encodings, which pack small values into a single byte —
  `0x80–0xFF` small block (tag in the low nibble, size in bits 4–6), `0x40–0x7F`
  small integer, `0x20–0x3F` small string;
- the explicit codes for larger values: `INT8/16/32/64`, `BLOCK32`, `BLOCK64`,
  `STRING8/32/64`, doubles in both endiannesses, double arrays, and custom
  blocks;
- **the sharing table.** This is the part that cannot be skipped. `Marshal`
  emits back-references to previously written objects rather than duplicating
  them, as `SHARED8/16/32/64` codes carrying a distance. The decoder must
  therefore append every block and string it creates to an ordered table, in
  creation order, and resolve references against it. A decoder that ignores
  sharing produces plausible output that is silently wrong.

### How the record layout was recovered

Field names being absent, the layout was recovered structurally and then
validated three ways.

**Structural recovery.** Printable runs in the stream were extracted in order,
which yields fragments such as a two-character string `AB`, a small integer `1`,
a two-character string `da`, and a nine-character string `PH 31a/17`. Read as a
block, this is the sign AB01, whose phonetic value is *da*, with a reference
attestation at PH 31a line 17 — a reading that is independently correct, which
confirms the parse.

**Cross-checking against the import source.** SigLA's import pipeline is
publicly available and declares the types. The label type carries a boolean
`unsure` beside the sign and its metadata, and the layer-option parser maps word
boundaries to a three-valued type — `Here`, `Unsure`, `Not_here` — rather than a
binary. Both were then located in the decoded structure at the predicted
positions.

**Validation against known facts.** The decoded corpus yields 802 documents and
5144 attestations, consistent with the interface's own reported document count.
Sign frequencies track an independent published count closely: sign A707 is
attested exactly 137 times in both this decoding and Corazza et al. And the
D4 adjudication (§4.2) agrees with a third editor at fifteen of fifteen sites, in
both directions — an outcome vanishingly unlikely from a mis-parsed structure.

A fourth, independent check became available after the decoder was built.
Salgarella and Castellan's 2021 paper describes SigLA's scope as administrative
documents only, while this decode's 802 records include 29–30 documents of
Z-series designation (libation tables, votive vessels, and similar religious
material). Partitioning the 802 records either by physical support (Tablet,
Nodule, Roundel, Sealing = 771) or by GORILA Z-designation (772 non-Z-type)
lands within a document or two of a figure never consulted while building the
decoder: Lamonica's chapter in the 2026 *Wor(l)ds of Linear A* volume states
SigLA holds "over 770 documents." Coverage grew, by almost exactly the
religious-document set this decoder recovers, sometime between 2021 and that
citation — a coincidence a parsing error would not produce.

### A finding from the format itself

The three-valued boundary encoding is not a detail. Our working corpus records
word division as a binary — a divider is present or absent — inherited from a
source that had no way to express uncertainty. SigLA distinguishes *definitely
here*, *uncertain*, and *definitely not here*.

Section 4.6 reports the consequence at KN Zc 6, where an unreadable sign sits
exactly at a contested word boundary that two editors resolved in opposite
directions with equal confidence. The uncertainty was in the source and was
flattened by both.
